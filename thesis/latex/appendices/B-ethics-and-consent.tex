\section{Institutional approval}
\label{app:approval}
The pilot runs in ordinary IIIT Surat lab classes with \emph{oral} institutional
approval from the Dean, the Head of the Department of Computer Science and
Engineering and the faculty who teach the classes. Written confirmation of that
approval is pending and will be attached here when it is issued. No student was
recruited for a separate study session: the thesis uses only recordings of
normal classes (\cref{sec:naturalistic}), the tool was used for teaching in
any case, and a flag only ever leads to the kind of conversation about code that
teachers have with students anyway.

The safeguards that the approval rests on are part of the system and are
described in \cref{sec:privacy}: a consent screen before any recording, no
camera, microphone or screen capture, raw keystrokes deleted after at most
28~days, erasure of a student's data on request, data kept in India, staff
access limited to their own classes, and analysis on pseudonymous aggregates
only. Spliced sessions used in the evaluation exist only inside the analysis and
are never shown to a teacher or attached to either student
(\cref{sec:splicing}).

\section{Consent text}
\label{app:consent}
Students see the following text, in full, on first sign-in and before any
event is recorded (\cref{fig:editor}a shows the screen). They tick ``I have read
this and agree to take part.'' and press \emph{Start}; the time and the version
of the text they agreed to are stored. A student who agreed to an earlier
version is asked again before their next session.

\begin{quote}\small
\textbf{Before you start}

This editor is part of a research study on how people write code. While you
work it records:
\begin{itemize}[nosep]
  \item When each key is pressed and released (timing, and which key)
  \item Every edit to your code
  \item Anything you paste, copy or cut in the editor
  \item Each time you run your program, with its output
  \item When you switch away from the editor or this tab
\end{itemize}

During a lab your teacher can follow the class live and open a read-only view
of your editor. The editor always tells you when they do, with their name.
Nothing from a live view is ever used to mark your work.

It does \textbf{not} use your webcam or microphone and does not record your
screen or other apps.

Your typing patterns are compared only with your own earlier sessions. If
something looks unusual, the only consequence is a conversation with your
instructor, never an automatic penalty. After such a conversation your
instructor may note how it went (for example ``explained, own work''). Only
staff see this note, it is never a mark, and the research reports only
class-wide counts without names. Raw keystroke data is deleted once summary
statistics have been computed: when your four-week baseline is built, and never
kept longer than 28 days.

\emph{\textsquare\ I have read this and agree to take part.} \quad [Start]
\end{quote}

\paragraph{Versions.} Version~1 (from 26 October 2026) had the list of what is
recorded, what is not, and the comparison, conversation and deletion
paragraph. Version~2 (27 October 2026, with the live classroom view) added
the live-view paragraph; students who had agreed to version~1 were asked again.
The sentences on the conversation note were added on 25 November 2026 when the
note was introduced (\cref{sec:review-timeline}). They describe a record that
staff keep about their own conversations, not new data about the student, so
the version was not raised and no student was asked again; students who signed
in afterwards saw the new text. If the live view is limited to the class
overview, the live paragraph instead reads: ``During a lab your teacher can
follow the class live: who is working, on which question, and your runs and
checks (not your code). A notice in the editor tells you while they do.'' If it
is turned off, the paragraph is not shown.

\paragraph{Sandbox Labs (version 3).} Before a student's first Sandbox Lab in
the desktop app, the screen adds that in such a lab the app also records the
keys pressed in the lab's terminal (including in vim or nano), each command
run with its folder, result and last lines of output, pastes into the terminal,
files in the lab folder changed outside the editor, packages installed, the
names of websites the lab connects to (never what is sent), programs listening
on a port, and a copy of the lab folder; that it sees nothing outside the lab's
folder and container; and that terminal typing is compared only with the
student's own sessions and counts toward attendance. Sandbox Labs are outside
the evaluation in this thesis, and the version~3 wording is still awaiting
the author's final approval.

real data reports class-wide aggregates under pseudonyms, and screenshots of
